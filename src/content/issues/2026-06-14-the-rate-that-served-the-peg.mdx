> **Historical case study.** Unlike this publication's weekly issues, the figures
> below are drawn from published sources, listed in the sidebar.

Britain joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism on 8 October 1990, at a
central rate of DM 2.95 with a six per cent band, and cut base rate from 15 to 14
per cent on the same day. It left on the evening of 16 September 1992, having
that morning raised the rate from 10 to 12 per cent and, at a quarter past two,
announced a further rise to 15 per cent that it never implemented.

The day is remembered for the speculation. The more useful thing in it is the
interest rate, because the rate is where the cost was actually paid, and it was
being paid for two years before anyone called it a crisis.

## What moved

The rate, twice in six hours, and then in the opposite direction for four months.

Sterling's suspension came at about half past seven that evening. The 12 per cent
was reversed the following day, and from there the rate fell almost without pause
— to 9 per cent within a week, 8 in October, 7 in November, and 6 by late
January 1993. Six percentage points in four months.

Set that against the two preceding years. Inside the mechanism, with a floor to
defend and a Bundesbank tightening to import, the rate came down from 14 to 10 —
four points in twenty-three months, and every one of them contested.

## Why it moved

A currency peg is not a currency policy. It is a monetary policy, wearing a
currency's clothes.

Under a peg the domestic interest rate is not available for domestic conditions;
it is committed to defending the parity. Britain in 1990 had joined a mechanism
anchored to a Bundesbank that was, at exactly that moment, tightening to absorb
the fiscal cost of German reunification. So the UK imported a monetary stance
calibrated for a booming Germany into an economy in recession, and the only
instrument that could have addressed the recession was the one already spoken
for.

That is why the rate could not fall, and why unemployment kept rising while it
did not. The cost never showed up in the exchange rate — which was, by
construction, stable right up until it wasn't. It showed up in the interest rate,
where almost nobody was reading it as a cost.

The 15 per cent announcement is the tell. A rate that is announced and never
implemented is not a monetary decision at all; it is a signal that the
authorities have run out of signals. Markets priced it as such within hours.

## What to watch

Not the peg, which will look fine until the morning it does not. Watch **the gap
between the policy rate and the rate the domestic economy would justify**, and
watch how long it has been open.

A peg with a small gap can survive an enormous speculative attack. A peg with a
large gap does not need one — it merely needs someone to notice, because the
government's own incentive to defend it is already gone. By September 1992 the
gap was six points wide, and the four months that followed measured it precisely.
