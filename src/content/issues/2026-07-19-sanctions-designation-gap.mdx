Sanctions have three dates, and almost every piece of commentary collapses them
into one.

There is the **announcement date**, when a government says what it intends to
do. There is the **effective date**, when the measure enters into legal force —
usually after a wind-down period of thirty to ninety days during which existing
contracts may still be settled. And there is the **enforcement date**, which is
not a date at all but a gradient: the point at which banks, insurers and
counterparties actually begin refusing business, which can lead the effective
date by weeks or lag it by years.

## What moved

Liquid assets reprice on the announcement, because that is when the information
arrives and the holders who must sell are forced to act. Index exclusion rules
are mechanical; a mandate that prohibits holding designated paper generates
selling pressure that has nothing to do with anyone's view of the credit.

That first move is therefore not a forecast of economic damage. It is a
liquidity event driven by mandate compliance, and it frequently overshoots.

## Why it moved

The gap between effective and enforcement dates is where the actual economics
sit, and it is governed by something harder to model than legal text:
**counterparty risk appetite**.

A bank facing a potential secondary designation does not run a careful legal
analysis of whether a given transaction is permitted. It runs a much cruder
calculation about whether the revenue justifies the tail risk of losing dollar
clearing access. The result is systematic over-compliance — the practical
perimeter of a sanctions regime is consistently wider than its written one, and
it widens fastest in the jurisdictions with the most to lose.

This cuts the other way too. Where enforcement capacity is thin or the political
will is soft, the written perimeter can be dramatically wider than the real one.
Trade reroutes through intermediary jurisdictions, price differentials compress
back toward pre-sanction levels, and the measure becomes a tax on transaction
complexity rather than a genuine prohibition.

## What to watch

Not the announcement, and not the legal text. Watch the **price differential
between sanctioned and unsanctioned equivalents** of the same commodity, and
watch **trade volumes reported by the intermediary jurisdictions** rather than
by the target. When the differential narrows while the target's official export
volumes stay flat, enforcement is decaying — and that decay is visible in the
data long before it is visible in the politics.
