A canal closure is rarely a supply shock. It is a *distance* shock, and the two
behave differently in a portfolio.

Nothing stops being produced when carriers avoid the Suez Canal. Every container
still leaves Shanghai and still arrives in Rotterdam. It simply goes around the
Cape of Good Hope, which adds somewhere near 3,500 nautical miles and ten days
to the voyage. The cargo is not destroyed. It is delayed and made more
expensive.

## What moved

The headline number is the container rate, and it is the least interesting one.
Rates spike because effective capacity falls: if every ship spends ten more days
at sea, the same fleet completes fewer round trips per year. Roughly a fifth of
the world's container capacity can vanish from the market without a single
vessel being lost.

The faster and more revealing move is insurance. War-risk cover is repriced in
days, not quarters, and underwriters are pricing a probability of loss rather
than a cost of delay. When the premium on hull value jumps by a factor of thirty
while the freight rate has barely moved, the insurance market is telling you it
expects an actual attack — not merely a longer trip.

## Why it moved

The transmission into consumer prices is slow enough that most positioning gets
the timing wrong in both directions.

A retailer's landed cost is fixed at the point of contract, not the point of
arrival. Long-term freight contracts are typically renegotiated annually, so a
spot-rate spike in month one reaches the shelf price somewhere between month
four and month eight, and only for goods contracted after the spike. This is why
a dramatic freight chart in January and a barely-moved core goods CPI in March
are not a contradiction — they are the same event, observed at different points
in the pipeline.

Energy is the exception that proves the point. Crude reroutes as easily as
containers do, so the barrel is not lost and the price move is modest. But
liquefied natural gas is chartered on a much thinner fleet, and a diversion that
merely inconveniences container lines can strand LNG cargoes outright.

## What to watch

Transit counts, not rate indices. Daily vessel transits through the strait are
observable and hard to spin, and they turn before the rate does. When transits
recover while rates stay elevated, the market is charging for a risk that
operators have already stopped pricing — and that gap is where the trade is.
