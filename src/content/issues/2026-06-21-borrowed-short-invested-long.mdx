By every measure a ratings committee looked at in 1997, Korea was fine. Growth
had run above 7 per cent for a decade. The budget was close to balance. Public
debt was low, the savings rate was among the highest in the world, and the export
sector was genuinely competitive.

In December of that year it agreed a programme with the IMF worth about $58
billion, the largest then assembled, and the won had gone from around 890 to the
dollar to a low near 1,960.

Nothing in the first paragraph is wrong. The second paragraph happened anyway,
because solvency and liquidity are different questions and only one of them was
being asked.

## What moved

The currency, and then everything priced in it. But the variable that decided the
outcome had finished moving years earlier.

In 1993 Korea removed controls on short-term overseas borrowing by banks while
leaving longer-maturity borrowing more restricted — so the cheapest way for a
Korean institution to fund itself abroad became the shortest. Short-term external
debt went from about $40 billion in 1993 to roughly $98 billion by the end of
September 1997, by which point it was over half of all external liabilities.

Reserves did not follow. They rose from about $20 billion to $33 billion and then
stopped. By 1996 short-term claims were near 280 per cent of official reserves.

Then in October and November 1997, defending the won, the authorities spent about
$15 billion of what was left.

## Why it moved

A maturity mismatch is a bet that lenders will keep lending, made by someone who
does not get a vote.

Borrowing short to lend long is profitable precisely because short money is
cheap, and it is cheap because the lender can leave. Each individual loan is
small, sensible and secured. In aggregate they constitute a claim on the central
bank's reserves that can be presented all at once, and the decision to present it
is not taken by the borrower, the regulator, or the government.

This is why contagion from Thailand — an economy Korea traded with modestly and
resembled little — was sufficient. Nothing had to be true about Korea. A lender
deciding not to roll a ninety-day facility is not making a judgement about
Korean solvency; it is managing its own book, and every other lender is doing the
same arithmetic in the same week.

The 1997 crisis was therefore not a mispricing of Korean credit. It was a
mispricing of *liquidity* — of the option that lenders held and borrowers had
sold cheaply, without recording it anywhere.

## What to watch

**Short-term external debt as a multiple of usable reserves**, and the word
usable is load-bearing: reserves already committed in forward contracts or
deposited with banks' overseas branches cannot be spent twice. Korea's headline
reserves were around $30 billion in late 1997; the amount actually available fell
to a small fraction of that.

The ratio is published, it moves slowly, and it was above 200 per cent for two
full years before anything happened — which is the useful part. Unlike a
contagion date, a maturity mismatch gives years of notice. It simply gives it in
a statistic nobody trades on.
