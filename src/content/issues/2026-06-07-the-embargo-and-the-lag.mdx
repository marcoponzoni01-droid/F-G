On 17 October 1973, eleven days into the Yom Kippur War, the Arab members of
OPEC announced production cuts and an embargo on the United States. The posted
price of Arabian Light had been $2.90 a barrel. By January it was $11.65.

That much is the standard account, and it is the least useful part. What makes
1973 the founding case for everything this publication covers is not the size of
the move but its *distribution in time*. The same event produced three separate
crises — an energy crisis, a stock market crisis and an inflation crisis — and
each was reported as though it were its own weather.

## What moved

Three things, on three completely different schedules.

The barrel moved first and fastest: a 70 per cent increase announced on 16
October, and a quadrupling complete within ninety days. This is the part
everyone remembers, and it was over before most portfolios had reacted.

Equities took a year. The S&P 500 had already peaked in January 1973, before the
war; it ground down through 1974 and did not bottom until 3 October 1974, at
62.28, roughly 48 per cent below the high. An investor who had been told the oil
price in advance would still have been wrong about the timing by about eleven
months.

Consumer prices took longer still. US inflation was running near 8 per cent when
the embargo began and did not peak until December 1974, at 12.3 per cent year on
year — fourteen months later, and by then the embargo itself had been lifted for
nine months.

## Why it moved

The lags are not noise. Each one is a contract.

Crude repriced immediately because it is sold on a spot and posted-price basis,
and the sellers were the ones setting the number. Nothing sat between the
decision and the price.

Equities repriced slowly because what they were digesting was not the oil price
but its consequence for margins and for the discount rate, and both of those
arrive through earnings, which are reported quarterly and revised for a year.

Consumer prices lagged most because energy reaches a household through a chain of
regulated tariffs, annual contracts and administered pass-throughs, each of which
is reset on its own schedule. The embargo ended in March 1974. The inflation it
caused had not finished arriving.

The 1974 recession, in other words, was not a second event. It was the first one,
still being delivered.

## What to watch

The gap between the fast clock and the slow one. When a commodity shock has
fully repriced but the price indices have not yet moved, the temptation is to
conclude that the shock was absorbed. 1973 is the case against that conclusion —
the absorption was still fourteen months from finishing, and it was in the slow
clock, not the fast one, that the policy response was eventually made.

Which is the same reason this publication's issue on European gas storage argues
for watching inventory rather than the front-month price. The mechanism is
identical; only the decade is different.
