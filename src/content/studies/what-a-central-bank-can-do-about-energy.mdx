Whenever energy prices spike, two arguments arrive on the same day. One says the
central bank must raise rates because inflation is above target. The other says
raising rates is absurd, because higher rates do not produce a single additional
cubic metre of gas.

Both are correct about their own premise and both miss what the decision is
actually about.

## The distinction that resolves it

A supply shock raises the *price level*. Monetary policy governs the *rate of
change* in prices over the medium term. These are different quantities, and the
confusion between them is the whole argument.

Consider what a doubling of the gas price does mechanically. Energy enters the
consumer basket directly, so headline inflation jumps roughly in proportion to
energy's weight in the basket. Twelve months later, if the gas price merely
stays high rather than doubling again, that contribution falls out of the annual
comparison and headline inflation drops sharply — with no help from anyone. The
price level stays permanently higher. The inflation *rate* returns on its own.

So if the shock is genuinely one-off, a central bank that does nothing gets
almost the same twelve-month outcome as one that tightens hard, at considerably
lower cost in output. This is the strongest form of the do-nothing argument and
it is correct as far as it goes.

## Where it stops going

It stops when the shock stops being one-off, and that is not a property of the
shock. It is a property of what people do next.

The three panels move on different clocks, and the ordering is the mechanism.
Headline inflation is the energy price arriving in the basket, essentially
immediately. Core inflation is firms passing energy costs through into
everything they sell, which takes about a year because prices are reset
periodically rather than continuously. Wage growth moves last, because wages are
set in negotiations that happen annually at most, and the negotiation that
matters is the one held *after* a year of visibly higher prices.

That last panel is the entire policy question. If wage and price-setting absorb
the shock as a new baseline, the one-off increase becomes a persistent rate of
increase — and unwinding that costs far more output later than leaning against
it early. If they do not, the shock passes through and out.

## What a rate decision is really addressing

Nothing in the first panel. By the time a committee meets, the energy price is
already in the index and no instrument reaches backwards.

What tightening does is act on the third panel, through two channels. It slows
demand, which reduces firms' ability to pass costs through without losing volume.
And, more importantly and less measurably, it signals that the target still
binds — which is an input into every wage negotiation and every price reset in
the economy.

That second channel is why central banks sometimes raise rates into an obvious
supply shock while saying openly that they cannot affect its cause. They are not
confused. They are addressing the only part of the sequence still undetermined.

## What to watch

**Not headline inflation**, which is telling you about the past and will fall
mechanically on its own.

Watch the **gap between core and headline**. Headline falling while core keeps
rising is pass-through in progress — the shock is spreading rather than fading.

Then watch **negotiated wage settlements and inflation expectations at the
five-to-ten-year horizon**. Settlements are published, they lag by design, and
they are the closest thing there is to a direct reading of whether the shock has
been written into the economy's expectations.

If long-horizon expectations are anchored while headline is at nine per cent,
the shock is being absorbed and policy has already done its job. If they drift
while headline is falling, the comfortable number is the misleading one.
