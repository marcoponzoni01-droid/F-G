Red Sea reroutes and the long tail of a freight shock

When carriers abandon a canal, the bill does not arrive at the port. It arrives in the inflation print, two quarters later.

  • Supply chain
  • Middle East
  • Europe
  • Global
  • Freight
  • Brent
  • EUR

In short

  • War-risk premium repriced from 0.02% to 0.70% of hull value inside a week
  • Asia–Europe container rates peaked at 220, week 0 = 100, in week 4
  • Euro-area core goods inflation peaked in month 7 — seven months after the shock
  • Roughly 3,500 nautical miles and 10 days added to each voyage

Example issue. Placeholder copy written to show the layout and the taxonomy. The figures are illustrative. Delete the example issues before you publish for real.

A canal closure is rarely a supply shock. It is a distance shock, and the two behave differently in a portfolio.

Nothing stops being produced when carriers avoid the Suez Canal. Every container still leaves Shanghai and still arrives in Rotterdam. It simply goes around the Cape of Good Hope, which adds somewhere near 3,500 nautical miles and ten days to the voyage. The cargo is not destroyed. It is delayed and made more expensive.

What moved

The headline number is the container rate, and it is the least interesting one. Rates spike because effective capacity falls: if every ship spends ten more days at sea, the same fleet completes fewer round trips per year. Roughly a fifth of the world’s container capacity can vanish from the market without a single vessel being lost.

The faster and more revealing move is insurance. War-risk cover is repriced in days, not quarters, and underwriters are pricing a probability of loss rather than a cost of delay. When the premium on hull value jumps by a factor of thirty while the freight rate has barely moved, the insurance market is telling you it expects an actual attack — not merely a longer trip.

Why it moved

The transmission into consumer prices is slow enough that most positioning gets the timing wrong in both directions.

A retailer’s landed cost is fixed at the point of contract, not the point of arrival. Long-term freight contracts are typically renegotiated annually, so a spot-rate spike in month one reaches the shelf price somewhere between month four and month eight, and only for goods contracted after the spike. This is why a dramatic freight chart in January and a barely-moved core goods CPI in March are not a contradiction — they are the same event, observed at different points in the pipeline.

Energy is the exception that proves the point. Crude reroutes as easily as containers do, so the barrel is not lost and the price move is modest. But liquefied natural gas is chartered on a much thinner fleet, and a diversion that merely inconveniences container lines can strand LNG cargoes outright.

What to watch

Transit counts, not rate indices. Daily vessel transits through the strait are observable and hard to spin, and they turn before the rate does. When transits recover while rates stay elevated, the market is charging for a risk that operators have already stopped pricing — and that gap is where the trade is.

The designation gap: why sanctions price in before they bite

The market moves on the announcement. The economy moves on the enforcement. The distance between those two dates is where the mispricing lives.

  • Sanctions
  • Russia & CIS
  • United States
  • Europe
  • Sovereign Debt
  • FX
  • Credit
  • Commodities

In short

  • Designated barrels priced 18 points below their unsanctioned equivalent in month 1
  • The discount narrowed to 3 points by month 12, with nothing repealed
  • Wind-down periods of 30 to 90 days sit between announcement and legal effect
  • Enforcement is a gradient, not a date — it can lead by weeks or lag by years

Example issue. Placeholder copy written to show the layout and the taxonomy. The figures are illustrative. Delete the example issues before you publish for real.

Sanctions have three dates, and almost every piece of commentary collapses them into one.

There is the announcement date, when a government says what it intends to do. There is the effective date, when the measure enters into legal force — usually after a wind-down period of thirty to ninety days during which existing contracts may still be settled. And there is the enforcement date, which is not a date at all but a gradient: the point at which banks, insurers and counterparties actually begin refusing business, which can lead the effective date by weeks or lag it by years.

What moved

Liquid assets reprice on the announcement, because that is when the information arrives and the holders who must sell are forced to act. Index exclusion rules are mechanical; a mandate that prohibits holding designated paper generates selling pressure that has nothing to do with anyone’s view of the credit.

That first move is therefore not a forecast of economic damage. It is a liquidity event driven by mandate compliance, and it frequently overshoots.

Why it moved

The gap between effective and enforcement dates is where the actual economics sit, and it is governed by something harder to model than legal text: counterparty risk appetite.

A bank facing a potential secondary designation does not run a careful legal analysis of whether a given transaction is permitted. It runs a much cruder calculation about whether the revenue justifies the tail risk of losing dollar clearing access. The result is systematic over-compliance — the practical perimeter of a sanctions regime is consistently wider than its written one, and it widens fastest in the jurisdictions with the most to lose.

This cuts the other way too. Where enforcement capacity is thin or the political will is soft, the written perimeter can be dramatically wider than the real one. Trade reroutes through intermediary jurisdictions, price differentials compress back toward pre-sanction levels, and the measure becomes a tax on transaction complexity rather than a genuine prohibition.

What to watch

Not the announcement, and not the legal text. Watch the price differential between sanctioned and unsanctioned equivalents of the same commodity, and watch trade volumes reported by the intermediary jurisdictions rather than by the target. When the differential narrows while the target’s official export volumes stay flat, enforcement is decaying — and that decay is visible in the data long before it is visible in the politics.

The substitution clock: what export controls actually buy

A control does not deny a capability. It prices delay — and the market almost always misprices how long the delay lasts.

  • Trade policy
  • China
  • United States
  • Japan & Korea
  • Equities
  • Commodities

In short

  • Domestic share of the controlled input overtook imported share at quarter 5
  • Imported share fell from 92% to 32% across eight quarters
  • Qualification announcements lead the share data by 2 to 3 quarters
  • Suppliers lost about a quarter of market capitalisation in the first week

Example issue. Placeholder copy written to show the layout and the taxonomy. The figures are illustrative. Delete the example issues before you publish for real.

An export control is usually reported as a denial. It is better understood as a tax on time.

Almost nothing on a control list is physically unobtainable. It is obtainable more slowly, from fewer suppliers, at worse yields, and at a price that includes the cost of proving provenance. The controlled party does not stop; it substitutes. So the only question that has ever mattered is how long substitution takes — and that is the one number nobody puts in the announcement.

What moved

Equities repriced on the announcement as though the denial were permanent. Suppliers of the controlled input lost a quarter of their market capitalisation in a week; the domestic champions expected to replace them gained roughly as much.

Both moves assumed the same thing — that the control holds. The first assumed it holds against the exporter’s revenue; the second assumed it holds long enough for a domestic replacement to reach volume. They cannot both be right for long, because the second is a statement that substitution is fast and the first is a statement that it is slow.

Why it moved

The substitution curve is slow at the start and then abruptly is not, which is exactly the shape that defeats linear extrapolation.

For the first several quarters the domestic share barely moves, because the constraint is not willingness or capital but qualification: a customer has to re-test the part, re-certify the process and accept the yield loss. That work is invisible in trade data. Then qualification completes across several customers at once, and share moves in a step rather than a drift.

This is why a control looks devastating for four quarters and porous by the eighth. Nothing changed about the policy in between. What changed is that the qualification pipeline emptied onto the market.

What to watch

Not export volumes, which are noisy and easily rerouted, but qualification announcements — the unglamorous customer disclosures that a second source has been approved for production. They lead the share data by two to three quarters and they are published, because customers have to tell their own investors.

When qualifications cluster while the price premium on the controlled input is still elevated, the premium is being paid for a denial that has already stopped being one.

The election premium is paid before the vote

Sovereign spreads widen through a campaign and compress on the result — very often regardless of who wins.

  • Elections
  • Latin America
  • Global
  • Sovereign Debt
  • FX
  • USD

In short

  • The sovereign spread widened almost 200bp across the campaign, peaking at 405bp
  • The peak fell on the first round — 6 weeks before the result
  • 75% of the widening was given back within a month
  • The currency, unlike the spread, does not mean-revert

Example issue. Placeholder copy written to show the layout and the taxonomy. The figures are illustrative. Delete the example issues before you publish for real.

The standard account of an election trade is that the market prefers one candidate and reprices when that candidate wins. The data rarely cooperates.

What reprices most reliably is not the identity of the winner but the width of the distribution. A two-way race with a five-point gap carries a smaller premium than a four-way race with a three-point gap, whatever the candidates propose. The premium is paid for not knowing, and it is refunded when the not-knowing ends.

What moved

The spread widened by nearly two hundred basis points across the campaign and gave back three-quarters of that within a month — with the turn arriving at the first round, not at the final result.

That timing is the whole tell. If the market were pricing a programme, the move would come when the programme became certain. It came instead when the field narrowed from four plausible outcomes to two, which is a statement about variance rather than about policy.

Why it moved

Mandates, not opinions, do most of the work here.

A fund with a volatility budget reduces exposure as realised and implied volatility rise, irrespective of its view on the election. Index rebalancing is mechanical. And local banks, which hold the largest share of most emerging sovereign curves, shorten duration into political uncertainty because their regulators ask them to. None of that selling is a forecast; all of it is unwound automatically once volatility falls.

The currency behaves differently, and this is where the two get conflated. The spread prices uncertainty and mean-reverts. The currency prices the expected policy mix and often does not come back, because a genuine change in the fiscal path is a change in the level, not the variance.

What to watch

The gap between the spread compression date and the inauguration date. When the spread has already retraced most of its campaign widening before the new government takes office, the market has priced the end of uncertainty and not the content of the programme — which means the actual fiscal announcements are still ahead of it, and they are not in the price.

Storage is the constraint, not price

The gas price tells you about today. The storage trajectory tells you about February — and only one of them is in the forwards.

  • Energy
  • Europe
  • United Kingdom
  • Natural Gas
  • Rates

In short

  • Storage peaked at 95% of capacity in week 26 and ended the season at 31%
  • The five-year average season low is 34%
  • Above roughly 90% fill a cold winter is absorbed; below 80% it is rationed by price
  • The injection season ran about 9 percentage points behind its norm

Example issue. Placeholder copy written to show the layout and the taxonomy. The figures are illustrative. Delete the example issues before you publish for real.

Every winter produces a run of stories about the gas price. Almost none of them are about the thing that decides whether the winter matters.

Gas is not consumed from production; it is consumed from storage, refilled across the summer and drawn down across the winter. The spot price is a claim on the marginal molecule today. The storage level is a claim on how many molecules exist between now and March, and it is the second one that determines whether a cold February is an inconvenience or a crisis.

What moved

Very little, which is the point. The front-month contract traded in a narrow range all summer while the injection season ran roughly nine percentage points behind its five-year norm.

A quiet price alongside a deteriorating inventory path is not a contradiction. Injection demand is price-inelastic in the short run — storage operators refill because they are obliged to, not because the price is attractive — so a shortfall shows up as a quantity first and only becomes a price once the shortfall is large enough to threaten the withdrawal season.

Why it moved

The transmission from inventory to interest rates is short, and shorter than most people expect, because European gas sits directly in the consumer price index rather than at the end of a long industrial chain.

A retail energy tariff is typically reset from a wholesale reference over a lagged window of several months. That means an autumn storage scare reaches the tariff — and therefore the headline inflation print — in the first quarter of the following year, arriving precisely when a central bank is deciding whether the previous year’s disinflation was durable.

This is why an energy story is so often really a rates story. The gas market moves the CPI through a fixed lag, and the fixed lag lands the shock in the middle of a policy meeting.

What to watch

The injection rate against the five-year band, weekly, through the summer — not the price. Storage that is filling slowly in July has already decided something about February that the forward curve will not acknowledge until October.

The specific level to watch is the end-of-injection fill. Above roughly ninety per cent, a cold winter is absorbed by inventory and the price barely responds. Below eighty, the same winter has to be rationed by price — and that is the version that reaches the inflation print, and the rate decision after it.

Korea 1997: solvent, and out of dollars anyway

The debt was not too large. It was too short — and a maturity mismatch does not need a recession to become a default.

  • Sovereign debt
  • Japan & Korea
  • South & Southeast Asia
  • Global
  • FX
  • Sovereign Debt
  • Credit

In short

  • Short-term external debt rose from $40bn in 1993 to $98bn by September 1997
  • Reserves stalled near $33bn — short-term claims reached about 280% of them by 1996
  • The won fell from around 890 to 1,960 per dollar
  • The IMF programme, agreed 3 December 1997, was worth about $58bn

Historical case study. Unlike this publication’s weekly issues, the figures below are drawn from published sources, listed in the sidebar.

By every measure a ratings committee looked at in 1997, Korea was fine. Growth had run above 7 per cent for a decade. The budget was close to balance. Public debt was low, the savings rate was among the highest in the world, and the export sector was genuinely competitive.

In December of that year it agreed a programme with the IMF worth about $58 billion, the largest then assembled, and the won had gone from around 890 to the dollar to a low near 1,960.

Nothing in the first paragraph is wrong. The second paragraph happened anyway, because solvency and liquidity are different questions and only one of them was being asked.

What moved

The currency, and then everything priced in it. But the variable that decided the outcome had finished moving years earlier.

In 1993 Korea removed controls on short-term overseas borrowing by banks while leaving longer-maturity borrowing more restricted — so the cheapest way for a Korean institution to fund itself abroad became the shortest. Short-term external debt went from about $40 billion in 1993 to roughly $98 billion by the end of September 1997, by which point it was over half of all external liabilities.

Reserves did not follow. They rose from about $20 billion to $33 billion and then stopped. By 1996 short-term claims were near 280 per cent of official reserves.

Then in October and November 1997, defending the won, the authorities spent about $15 billion of what was left.

Why it moved

A maturity mismatch is a bet that lenders will keep lending, made by someone who does not get a vote.

Borrowing short to lend long is profitable precisely because short money is cheap, and it is cheap because the lender can leave. Each individual loan is small, sensible and secured. In aggregate they constitute a claim on the central bank’s reserves that can be presented all at once, and the decision to present it is not taken by the borrower, the regulator, or the government.

This is why contagion from Thailand — an economy Korea traded with modestly and resembled little — was sufficient. Nothing had to be true about Korea. A lender deciding not to roll a ninety-day facility is not making a judgement about Korean solvency; it is managing its own book, and every other lender is doing the same arithmetic in the same week.

The 1997 crisis was therefore not a mispricing of Korean credit. It was a mispricing of liquidity — of the option that lenders held and borrowers had sold cheaply, without recording it anywhere.

What to watch

Short-term external debt as a multiple of usable reserves, and the word usable is load-bearing: reserves already committed in forward contracts or deposited with banks’ overseas branches cannot be spent twice. Korea’s headline reserves were around $30 billion in late 1997; the amount actually available fell to a small fraction of that.

The ratio is published, it moves slowly, and it was above 200 per cent for two full years before anything happened — which is the useful part. Unlike a contagion date, a maturity mismatch gives years of notice. It simply gives it in a statistic nobody trades on.

  • World Bank — The Korean Financial Crisis of 1997 ↗
  • BIS — Policy responses to strengthen liquidity risk management in Korea ↗

Black Wednesday: the price of a rate that served the peg

Britain raised its base rate twice in six hours to defend a currency floor, then left the floor and cut six points in four months. The economy the rate was supposed to serve had been waiting two years.

  • Monetary policy
  • United Kingdom
  • Europe
  • Rates
  • FX
  • Equities

In short

  • Base rate went 10% to 12%, and 15% was announced, within six hours on 16 September 1992
  • The 15% was never implemented; sterling left the ERM that evening
  • The rate fell 6 points in the 4 months after leaving
  • It had fallen only 4 points in the 23 months inside

Historical case study. Unlike this publication’s weekly issues, the figures below are drawn from published sources, listed in the sidebar.

Britain joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism on 8 October 1990, at a central rate of DM 2.95 with a six per cent band, and cut base rate from 15 to 14 per cent on the same day. It left on the evening of 16 September 1992, having that morning raised the rate from 10 to 12 per cent and, at a quarter past two, announced a further rise to 15 per cent that it never implemented.

The day is remembered for the speculation. The more useful thing in it is the interest rate, because the rate is where the cost was actually paid, and it was being paid for two years before anyone called it a crisis.

What moved

The rate, twice in six hours, and then in the opposite direction for four months.

Sterling’s suspension came at about half past seven that evening. The 12 per cent was reversed the following day, and from there the rate fell almost without pause — to 9 per cent within a week, 8 in October, 7 in November, and 6 by late January 1993. Six percentage points in four months.

Set that against the two preceding years. Inside the mechanism, with a floor to defend and a Bundesbank tightening to import, the rate came down from 14 to 10 — four points in twenty-three months, and every one of them contested.

Why it moved

A currency peg is not a currency policy. It is a monetary policy, wearing a currency’s clothes.

Under a peg the domestic interest rate is not available for domestic conditions; it is committed to defending the parity. Britain in 1990 had joined a mechanism anchored to a Bundesbank that was, at exactly that moment, tightening to absorb the fiscal cost of German reunification. So the UK imported a monetary stance calibrated for a booming Germany into an economy in recession, and the only instrument that could have addressed the recession was the one already spoken for.

That is why the rate could not fall, and why unemployment kept rising while it did not. The cost never showed up in the exchange rate — which was, by construction, stable right up until it wasn’t. It showed up in the interest rate, where almost nobody was reading it as a cost.

The 15 per cent announcement is the tell. A rate that is announced and never implemented is not a monetary decision at all; it is a signal that the authorities have run out of signals. Markets priced it as such within hours.

What to watch

Not the peg, which will look fine until the morning it does not. Watch the gap between the policy rate and the rate the domestic economy would justify, and watch how long it has been open.

A peg with a small gap can survive an enormous speculative attack. A peg with a large gap does not need one — it merely needs someone to notice, because the government’s own incentive to defend it is already gone. By September 1992 the gap was six points wide, and the four months that followed measured it precisely.

October 1973: the shock that took a year to arrive

Crude quadrupled in ninety days. The equity trough came a year later and the inflation peak fourteen months later — the same event, arriving three times.

  • Conflict
  • Middle East
  • United States
  • Europe
  • Commodities
  • Equities
  • Rates

In short

  • The posted price went from $2.90 to $11.65 a barrel in about 90 days
  • The S&P 500 bottomed at 62.28 on 3 October 1974, some 48% below its high
  • US inflation peaked at 12.3% in December 1974 — 14 months after the embargo
  • 11 months separate the oil peak from the equity low

Historical case study. Unlike this publication’s weekly issues, the figures below are drawn from published sources, listed in the sidebar.

On 17 October 1973, eleven days into the Yom Kippur War, the Arab members of OPEC announced production cuts and an embargo on the United States. The posted price of Arabian Light had been $2.90 a barrel. By January it was $11.65.

That much is the standard account, and it is the least useful part. What makes 1973 the founding case for everything this publication covers is not the size of the move but its distribution in time. The same event produced three separate crises — an energy crisis, a stock market crisis and an inflation crisis — and each was reported as though it were its own weather.

What moved

Three things, on three completely different schedules.

The barrel moved first and fastest: a 70 per cent increase announced on 16 October, and a quadrupling complete within ninety days. This is the part everyone remembers, and it was over before most portfolios had reacted.

Equities took a year. The S&P 500 had already peaked in January 1973, before the war; it ground down through 1974 and did not bottom until 3 October 1974, at 62.28, roughly 48 per cent below the high. An investor who had been told the oil price in advance would still have been wrong about the timing by about eleven months.

Consumer prices took longer still. US inflation was running near 8 per cent when the embargo began and did not peak until December 1974, at 12.3 per cent year on year — fourteen months later, and by then the embargo itself had been lifted for nine months.

Why it moved

The lags are not noise. Each one is a contract.

Crude repriced immediately because it is sold on a spot and posted-price basis, and the sellers were the ones setting the number. Nothing sat between the decision and the price.

Equities repriced slowly because what they were digesting was not the oil price but its consequence for margins and for the discount rate, and both of those arrive through earnings, which are reported quarterly and revised for a year.

Consumer prices lagged most because energy reaches a household through a chain of regulated tariffs, annual contracts and administered pass-throughs, each of which is reset on its own schedule. The embargo ended in March 1974. The inflation it caused had not finished arriving.

The 1974 recession, in other words, was not a second event. It was the first one, still being delivered.

What to watch

The gap between the fast clock and the slow one. When a commodity shock has fully repriced but the price indices have not yet moved, the temptation is to conclude that the shock was absorbed. 1973 is the case against that conclusion — the absorption was still fourteen months from finishing, and it was in the slow clock, not the fast one, that the policy response was eventually made.

Which is the same reason this publication’s issue on European gas storage argues for watching inventory rather than the front-month price. The mechanism is identical; only the decade is different.

Italy: the debt, the banks and the spread

A debt stock that size is not a solvency question. It is a rollover question, and the answer is written on the balance sheets of the banks that hold it.

Updated 30 July 2026 · first published 24 May 2026

  • Sovereign debt
  • Monetary policy
  • Elections
  • Europe
  • Sovereign Debt
  • Credit
  • Equities
  • Rates

Example study. Placeholder copy written to show the format. Figures are indicative and the sources are listed for the structure, not for the numbers.

Every few years an Italian political event produces the same headline: the spread is widening. Almost none of the coverage explains what the spread is measuring, which is a pity, because it is measuring something quite specific and it is not the government’s ability to pay.

Italy’s debt is around one and a half times national output. That number, on its own, tells you very little. Japan’s is higher and nothing happens; Greece’s was lower when everything happened. What matters is not the stock but three things about its structure: how long it is, what currency it is in, and who holds it.

What the structure is

The debt is long. Average residual maturity sits around seven years, which means that in any given year the Treasury has to refinance roughly a seventh of it, not all of it. A rate shock therefore arrives slowly — the average cost of the stock moves a fraction as fast as the marginal cost of new issuance. This is the single most under-appreciated fact about Italian debt, and it is why a violent move in yields does not translate into a fiscal crisis for years, if ever.

The debt is in euros, which the Italian state does not issue. That is the genuine vulnerability, and it is categorically different from the first one. A country that borrows in its own currency can always pay nominally; the question is only what the money is worth. A euro-area sovereign can actually run out. Everything that looks like credit risk in an Italian bond is, at bottom, this.

And the debt is held mostly at home.

Why that last fact does the work

The domestic holding share is what turns a funding problem into a banking problem, and it is the mechanism people mean when they say doom loop without usually saying how it works.

When foreign investors reduce exposure, Italian banks and insurers buy. They are regulated in ways that make holding domestic sovereign debt attractive — it carries a zero risk weight in the standardised approach, and it is the natural collateral for central bank operations. So the auctions clear. There is no dramatic failure.

What happens instead is that the banking system’s assets become more correlated with the sovereign’s fortunes at exactly the moment the sovereign’s fortunes are in question. If the spread widens, banks take mark-to-market losses, their capital ratios tighten, and they lend less to Italian firms. Weaker firms mean weaker growth, weaker growth means a worse debt path, and a worse debt path widens the spread. The loop closes.

The spread, then, is not the market’s estimate of default probability. It is closer to a live reading of how much of that loop the market thinks is currently running — and of how credible the backstop is.

The backstop, and its condition

Since 2012 the answer to “who stops the loop” has been the ECB, and the important detail is that its instruments are conditional. Support is available to a country that is following agreed fiscal rules; it is not available unconditionally. That conditionality is what makes an Italian election a market event, because what is being priced is not the winner’s spending plans in themselves but whether those plans keep the backstop available.

This is why Italian spreads often move before a policy is announced and settle after the government has been formed, regardless of who formed it — the same pattern the weekly issue on election premia describes in a different market.

What to watch

Three things, in order of how much they tell you:

  1. The domestic bank holding share. Rising while the spread widens is the loop tightening. Rising while the spread is calm is just carry.
  2. Average residual maturity. Shortening means the Treasury is being pushed down the curve to fund, and it converts a slow problem into a fast one.
  3. The gap between the ten-year spread and the two-year spread. Widening at the short end is the market pricing something imminent — redenomination or a liquidity event. Widening only at the long end is a growth story, which is less alarming and much more common.

The thing that is not worth watching is the debt-to-GDP ratio in isolation. It moves slowly, it is revised, and by the time it has told you anything the other three have been shouting for a year.

What a central bank can and cannot do about an energy shock

Rates cannot produce gas. What they can do is decide whether one expensive winter becomes a decade of expectations — and that is a different job from the one central banks are usually accused of failing at.

Published 30 June 2026

  • Monetary policy
  • Energy
  • Supply chain
  • Europe
  • United States
  • Global
  • Rates
  • Natural Gas
  • Equities
  • FX

Example study. Placeholder copy written to show the format. The profiles in the chart are stylised and labelled as such.

Whenever energy prices spike, two arguments arrive on the same day. One says the central bank must raise rates because inflation is above target. The other says raising rates is absurd, because higher rates do not produce a single additional cubic metre of gas.

Both are correct about their own premise and both miss what the decision is actually about.

The distinction that resolves it

A supply shock raises the price level. Monetary policy governs the rate of change in prices over the medium term. These are different quantities, and the confusion between them is the whole argument.

Consider what a doubling of the gas price does mechanically. Energy enters the consumer basket directly, so headline inflation jumps roughly in proportion to energy’s weight in the basket. Twelve months later, if the gas price merely stays high rather than doubling again, that contribution falls out of the annual comparison and headline inflation drops sharply — with no help from anyone. The price level stays permanently higher. The inflation rate returns on its own.

So if the shock is genuinely one-off, a central bank that does nothing gets almost the same twelve-month outcome as one that tightens hard, at considerably lower cost in output. This is the strongest form of the do-nothing argument and it is correct as far as it goes.

Where it stops going

It stops when the shock stops being one-off, and that is not a property of the shock. It is a property of what people do next.

The three panels move on different clocks, and the ordering is the mechanism. Headline inflation is the energy price arriving in the basket, essentially immediately. Core inflation is firms passing energy costs through into everything they sell, which takes about a year because prices are reset periodically rather than continuously. Wage growth moves last, because wages are set in negotiations that happen annually at most, and the negotiation that matters is the one held after a year of visibly higher prices.

That last panel is the entire policy question. If wage and price-setting absorb the shock as a new baseline, the one-off increase becomes a persistent rate of increase — and unwinding that costs far more output later than leaning against it early. If they do not, the shock passes through and out.

What a rate decision is really addressing

Nothing in the first panel. By the time a committee meets, the energy price is already in the index and no instrument reaches backwards.

What tightening does is act on the third panel, through two channels. It slows demand, which reduces firms’ ability to pass costs through without losing volume. And, more importantly and less measurably, it signals that the target still binds — which is an input into every wage negotiation and every price reset in the economy.

That second channel is why central banks sometimes raise rates into an obvious supply shock while saying openly that they cannot affect its cause. They are not confused. They are addressing the only part of the sequence still undetermined.

What to watch

Not headline inflation, which is telling you about the past and will fall mechanically on its own.

Watch the gap between core and headline. Headline falling while core keeps rising is pass-through in progress — the shock is spreading rather than fading.

Then watch negotiated wage settlements and inflation expectations at the five-to-ten-year horizon. Settlements are published, they lag by design, and they are the closest thing there is to a direct reading of whether the shock has been written into the economy’s expectations.

If long-horizon expectations are anchored while headline is at nine per cent, the shock is being absorbed and policy has already done its job. If they drift while headline is falling, the comfortable number is the misleading one.

Open structural questions

Things worth settling before any of this is re-encoded in components. The first two are expensive to change later; the rest are cheap.

  1. Content model

    Is an issue one topic, or one week?

    Right now an issue has a single title, dek and eventType — that models a single-subject essay. Most weeklies carry three to five stories. If that is the intent, an issue needs segments, each with its own tags and market moves, and the archive has to decide whether it searches issues or the stories inside them. This reaches the schema, the archive and the card design at once.

  2. Scope of the database

    Does the archive hold more than back-issues?

    Your brief said the database stores "all the information about the past". That may be wider than a pile of past newsletters. If evergreen event dossiers — written once, not tied to any week — belong there too, that is a second content type and a second card, not a tweak.

  3. Navigation

    Should the archive be the front door?

    The nav reads This week → Archive → About. But the archive is the durable product and the newsletter is the weekly pulse into it. A returning reader may want the record first.

  4. Archive contents

    Does the archive include the current issue?

    It does today, badged "Current issue", so someone searching for this week still finds it. Flip site.archiveIncludesCurrent to restrict it strictly to past issues.

  5. Filters

    One value per facet, or several?

    Region, asset and type are single-select, which keeps the URL clean and the mental model simple. Multi-select answers "Europe or Middle East" but needs a chip UI and a longer query string.

  6. Archive cards

    Should the whole card be clickable?

    Only the title is a link today, so a card is a small target — and on a two-line title there is even a dead strip between the lines that does nothing. Making the whole card clickable (a stretched link over the card) is a few lines and a meaningfully better target, especially on a phone.

  7. Charts \u00b7 decided

    Three chart types, reused

    transmission-lag for sequence, divergence for a spread that opens or closes, event-track for a calendar. Each is used at least twice across the five issues, which is what makes this a component rather than a weekly drawing job. A fourth type should have to earn its place.

  8. Market moves

    Do the story-specific movers come back?

    The panel is a standing five-benchmark dashboard now, comparable week to week. But it displaced the rows that carried the argument — the Asia–Europe container rate, the war-risk premium, the designated-sector credit line. A second block beneath the standing panel would give you both, at the cost of one more field in the schema.

  9. Issue page

    Sidebar or inline?

    Market moves and sources sit in a sidebar that collapses under the article on mobile. Inline puts them in reading order — stronger on a phone, weaker as a reference you scan back to.

  10. Missing fields

    Byline and corrections.

    There is no author field, which matters the moment anyone else writes an issue. And the About page promises corrections appear in a later issue, but nothing links a correction to the piece it corrects.
